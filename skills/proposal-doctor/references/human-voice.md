# Voice check: does it read like a person wrote it?

## What this is for, and what it is not

Clients do not run detectors. They skim a list and, in about two seconds each, skip
anything that sounds like the rest of the pile. The pile is mostly machine-written, so the
patterns below are what "skip" looks like.

The aim is a letter that is truly the freelancer's: their facts, their opinion, their way of
saying it, with the machine habits edited out. It is not to disguise a letter nobody
thought about. So:

- No fake typos, no forced slang, no lowercase gimmicks. They read worse and they are a lie.
- No "I wrote this myself" lines. Announcing it lowers replies and invites doubt.
- No swapping words for synonyms to dodge a list. If a sentence is empty, cut it.

The most reliable way to pass every check here is upstream: get the freelancer's own rough
take first (see `writing-the-proposal.md`) and edit that, instead of generating and then
sanding.

## 1. Instant giveaways (never send with these)

- A link carrying `utm_source=chatgpt.com`, `utm_source=claude` or similar. The tool tagged
  the link and the client can read it.
- Placeholders: `[Client Name]`, `[Your Name]`, `{project}`, `XX`.
- Chatbot leftovers: "Here's a revised version", "I hope this helps", "Let me know if you'd
  like me to adjust the tone", "Certainly!".
- Markdown: `**bold**`, `# headings`, `[text](link)`. Upwork prints the symbols.
- Section headers or bold labels in a letter ("Approach:", "Why me:", "Timeline:").
- Following an AI trap in the post, or missing a code word meant for humans.
- Curly quotes and apostrophes throughout. Typed text has straight ones; curly ones mean a
  paste from a chatbot or word processor. Weak alone, telling next to other signs.

## 2. Stock phrases

Each of these could sit in any proposal, which is exactly why clients skip them.

| Cut | Do this instead |
|---|---|
| I hope this message finds you well | Start with the point |
| I came across / I read your job post | Use a detail from it |
| I'm excited / thrilled / interested | Say something specific about the job |
| I'm the perfect fit / ideal candidate | Give the one proof that makes it true |
| I have X years of experience / extensive experience | Name the project |
| I have experience with... | "I built...", "Last month I..." |
| I understand that you need... | State what their need implies |
| proven track record, high-quality work, 100% satisfaction | One result |
| tailored to your needs, bring your vision to life, next level | Nothing. Cut. |
| Would you be open to a quick call? | A question about the work |
| Feel free to reach out / Looking forward to hearing from you | End on the question |
| Best regards / Kind regards / Sincerely | First name, or nothing |
| Dear Sir / Hiring Manager / Hi there / Greetings | Their first name if known, else no greeting |
| Unlike other freelancers... | Talk about the job |

## 3. Words that sound generated

delve, leverage, robust, seamless, pivotal, crucial, comprehensive, meticulous, streamline,
elevate, foster, harness, unlock, empower, showcase, underscore, testament, tapestry,
landscape, realm, holistic, bespoke, synergy, cutting-edge, state-of-the-art, top-notch,
world-class, innovative, utilize, facilitate, intricate, multifaceted, moreover,
furthermore, additionally, ever-evolving, fast-paced, user-centric, game-changer,
impactful, actionable, effortlessly.

Use the plain word: use, build, fix, strong, important, careful, also. One exception: if
the client used the word in their post, mirroring it is fine.

## 4. Sentence shapes

These are harder to see than words and matter more. Any one of them once is ordinary
English. The tell is the pattern repeating.

- **Contrast framing.** "This isn't just X, it's Y." "It's a preservation problem, not a
  rewrite." "As an assistant, not an autopilot." "X rather than Y." It argues with a
  position nobody took. One per letter at most. Say the positive claim directly.
- **Threes.** "Fast, reliable and scalable." "Design, build and deploy." Real lists have two
  items, or four, or one.
- **Trailing -ing clause.** "..., ensuring a smooth experience." "..., making it easy to
  maintain." Cut the tail or make it its own sentence with a fact in it.
- **Colon reveal.** "The result: a faster store." "My approach: start small."
- **Question and answer.** "The best part? It's fully automated."
- **Even rhythm.** Every sentence 12 to 20 words, every paragraph three sentences. People
  write a long one and then a short one.
- **Tidy arc.** Intro, three matched body paragraphs, a summary line that restates the
  opening. A message stops when it has said what it has to say.
- **Em dashes and semicolons.** Rare in typed messages. Use a comma, a full stop, brackets.
- **Hedges on everything.** "Typically", "generally", "can often". Keep a hedge only where
  the code or files have not been seen.

## 5. Tone

- **Inflated enthusiasm and flattery.** "What a fantastic project!" Nobody talks like this
  to a stranger about their bug.
- **Corporate "we"** from a solo freelancer.
- **Over-politeness.** "Kindly", "I would be honoured", "at your earliest convenience".
  ("Kindly", "do the needful" and "Greetings of the day" are ordinary English in some
  regions, but many clients associate them with bulk bids. Plain "please" is safer.)
- **Compliance voice.** "These examples support the UI side of this brief; I am not
  presenting them as API case studies." "This does not by itself establish..." "My closest
  official example is..." This is the newest machine habit, and it also argues against the
  freelancer. Be accurate by choosing true statements, not by attaching disclaimers to them.
- **Self-undermining.** "I haven't worked extensively with...", "it isn't live anymore",
  "to be transparent...". One plain statement of a real gap is honest and human. Three is a
  letter asking to be declined.

## 6. What a person's letter has

Add these because they are true, not as decoration.

- Contractions: I'd, I've, it's, don't.
- One short sentence. Maybe two.
- Concrete nouns and real numbers from the freelancer's own work.
- An opinion with a cost attached: "I'd skip the slider. It costs more speed than it earns."
- Something only a practitioner knows, usually the thing that goes wrong.
- A time reference: "this week", "before Friday", "last month".
- Plain verbs: built, fixed, moved, cut, shipped.
- The freelancer's own turns of phrase from their rough take.
- "You" and "your" at least as often as "I" and "my".

## 7. Three tests

1. **Read it aloud.** Mark every place you would not say it that way to a client on a call.
   Rewrite those the way you would say them.
2. **Delete test.** Remove one sentence at a time. If the letter loses nothing, the sentence
   stays out.
3. **Colleague test.** Would you send this, as written, to a developer or designer you
   respect? If you would be embarrassed by a line, so will the client be.

## 8. Voice card

To write in a particular freelancer's voice, collect three things once and reuse them:

- two or three messages they typed themselves to clients (not AI-assisted)
- how they greet and sign off, and how formal they are
- words they use and words they never would

Note sentence length, how blunt they are, and their habits (starts with the answer, uses
"so", asks short questions). Keep the card with their proof bank. Check every draft against
it: would they say this?

## 9. Writers whose first language is not English

Do not polish the person away. Short, correct, plain English from someone who clearly knows
the work beats ornate English. Fix grammar that causes confusion; leave their directness
alone.
