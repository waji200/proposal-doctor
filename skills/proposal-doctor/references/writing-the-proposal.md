# Writing the proposal

A proposal is a short message from one working person to another. It has four jobs, in this
order: get opened, show the post was read, show the work can be done, make replying easy.

## Before drafting

Work these out first. Writing before this is how generic letters happen.

1. **The outcome in the client's words.** One sentence: what do they have at the end, and
   what is it for? "A store that loads fast on phones so ad traffic stops bouncing", not
   "speed optimisation".
2. **The fear.** What has gone wrong for them before, or could go wrong here? A broken live
   site, a developer who vanished, a budget that doubled. Posts usually hint at it.
3. **Two or three anchors.** Details only someone who read the whole post would mention: a
   number, a tool, a constraint, a deadline, something in an attachment.
4. **The must-haves.** Every question asked, every item requested, any code word.
5. **The freelancer's own take.** Ask: *"In your own words, two or three rough sentences:
   what's the tricky part of this job and what would you do first?"* Build the letter from
   that answer and keep their phrasing where it is clear. This is what makes the letter
   sound like them and carry knowledge a generic writer would not have. If they skip it,
   draft anyway and mark every technical claim they need to confirm.
6. **One proof.** Pick the single closest piece of past work. Same problem beats same
   industry, which beats same framework. It needs something checkable: a link, an attached
   item, a number, or a review.

## The first two sentences

These are the preview. Draft them first and judge them alone: would this client, reading
only this, recognise their own project and want the next line?

Openers that work:

- **A specific observation with a consequence.** Take one anchor and say what it means.
  "Fourteen apps on Dawn is almost certainly where your 28 comes from."
- **The closest proof, in past tense.** "Last month I moved a 300-page WordPress site to
  Next.js without losing a single ranking URL, which is the part of your migration I'd
  worry about most."
- **A sharp question that changes the scope.** "Is the Figma file final, or are screens
  still moving? It changes how I'd split the milestones."
- **A direct answer to the question they asked.** If the post asks "can this be done in two
  weeks?", start with "Yes, two weeks works if the API is stable" and why.

Use the client's first name only when the post or their reviews actually show it.

Openers to avoid, and why:

- **Who you are** ("I'm a full-stack developer with 6 years..."). The card already shows
  it, and the client is looking for their project, not yours.
- **Saying you read the post** ("I went through your requirements carefully"). Using a
  detail proves it; saying so proves nothing.
- **Echoing the brief** ("You need a responsive site with contact forms and SEO"). They
  know what they asked for, and any chatbot can paraphrase a post. An anchor has to be
  *used*: attach a risk, a decision or a first step to it.
- **Praise and enthusiasm** ("Great project!", "I'm excited to..."). Every bid has it.

## The body

Three short moves. Each can be a sentence or a small paragraph.

**What you'd do first, and what they'd have after it.** One concrete first step and the first
thing they receive. "I'd do the pass on a duplicate theme, so the live store isn't touched
until you've seen the numbers. Three working days." Give the first move and the reasoning,
not the whole manual. A full plan removes the client's reason to reply and is free
consulting for whoever they hire.

**The proof.** What it was, what you did, what happened, where to see it. One, two at most.
"Orbit, a Next.js web application" tells a client nothing. "The booking dashboard for a
chain of dental clinics: appointments, reminders, staff rotas. Live at [link]" tells them what
to look at and why it is relevant.

**Anything they asked for.** Availability, timezone overlap, a price basis. One line each.
Leave out what they did not ask for.

For fixed-price bids, say in one line what the bid covers and the main assumption ("$900
covers five pages from your finished Figma file"). Put milestone breakdowns in the milestone
fields, not in the letter.

## The close

End with one question that is easy to answer and moves the work forward. "Which two apps
could you not live without?" is easy. "Would you be open to a discovery call?" is a cost.
Sign with a first name, or nothing.

## Length and layout

- Default 80 to 150 words. Up to about 220 for a detailed expert brief. Mirror the client's
  effort: a three-line post gets 60 to 90 words.
- Plain text. Short paragraphs with a blank line between. No headings, no bold, no emoji.
- At most one short list (three items) and only when the client asked for several things.
- One or two links, clean, straight to the relevant thing. Strip tracking tags.

## Screening answers

- Answer 1 is often the first thing the client reads. Put the hook there.
- First sentence answers the question. Then one detail that shows it is real.
- Usually 40 to 120 words. Longer only when the question asks for a walkthrough.
- Never "see my cover letter". Each box may be read alone.
- Do not repeat the same project line in every answer. Use a different angle each time.
- If the true answer is "no", that is the job gate speaking. Skip the job.

## The demo move

For small, visual or proof-of-concept jobs with a serious client, the strongest proof is a
rough version of their thing: a 60-second Loom walking through their site or file, or a
quick prototype of the feature they described. Put it in the first line: "I built a rough
version of your expand-floors viewer this morning: [link]." It only counts if it is real and
about their job. Offer it or attach it; never claim one that does not exist.

## Worked example

Job post (shortened): *"Our Shopify store is painfully slow on mobile, PageSpeed 28. We sell
handmade candles, about 300 products, Dawn theme, 14 apps. Need someone to get us above 70
without breaking anything. Budget $400."*

**What most bids look like**

> Hi there, I hope you're doing well! I came across your job post and I'm excited to help.
> I am a seasoned Shopify expert with 7+ years of experience in speed optimization. I
> understand that you need to improve your store's performance, and I can leverage my
> expertise to deliver a seamless, robust solution tailored to your needs. My approach
> includes image optimization, code minification, and app audit, ensuring your store loads
> fast across all devices. I have a proven track record and 100% client satisfaction.
> Looking forward to hearing from you. Best regards

Nothing in it could not be pasted under any Shopify speed job. The preview is a greeting and
a claim of excitement. There is no proof, no first step and no question.

**A version that gets opened**

> Fourteen apps on Dawn is almost certainly where your 28 comes from. On stores like yours,
> most of the mobile score goes to app scripts that load on every page, including pages
> that never use them.
>
> Last month I took a 400-product Dawn store from 31 to 78 on mobile. I didn't remove
> anything the owner used. I deferred five app scripts, swapped the hero slider for a still
> image and lazy-loaded the collection grids. Before and after report attached.
>
> For $400 I'd do the same pass on a duplicate theme, so your live store isn't touched
> until you've seen the numbers. It takes me three working days.
>
> Which two or three apps could you not live without? I'll start with the others.

Why it works: the first sentence uses two of their numbers and says something they did not
know. The proof is the same problem, with figures and an attachment. The plan answers their
fear ("without breaking anything") with a specific safeguard. The close is a question they
can answer in ten seconds, and answering it starts the project.

Every fact in a letter like this has to be true for the freelancer sending it. If there is
no 31-to-78 project, the proof paragraph becomes whatever the closest real one is, stated as
plainly.

**Same job, weaker proof, still honest**

> Fourteen apps on Dawn is almost certainly where your 28 comes from. App scripts load on
> every page, including the ones that never use them.
>
> I haven't published a Shopify speed case study yet. What I can show is the same work on a
> Next.js store: 2.9s down to 1.1s on mobile, mostly by deferring third-party scripts.
> Report attached.
>
> I'd start on a duplicate theme and send you the before and after score for the home page
> within a day. If it hasn't moved, you've lost nothing.
>
> Which two or three apps could you not live without?

One plain sentence states the gap, then the letter moves on. That is different from a letter
that keeps apologising. If the gap is the main requirement, the job gate should have said
skip.
